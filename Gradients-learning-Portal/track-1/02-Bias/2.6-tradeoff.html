Lesson 2.2: The Bias-Variance Tradeoff

The bias-variance tradeoff is the fundamental balancing act in supervised learning. As we increase model complexity, bias decreases but variance increases. The optimal model is the one that minimizes the total expected error â€” the sum of squared bias, variance, and irreducible noise.

\text{Expected Error}(x) = \text{Bias}^2(x) + \text{Variance}(x) + \sigma^2

Understanding the Tradeoff

Consider three model families applied to the same non-linear problem:

  • Simple model (high bias, low variance): A linear regression fit to a curved relationship. Predictions are consistently off (high bias) but stable across different training datasets (low variance).
  • Moderate model (balanced): A polynomial of degree 3. It captures the curve well enough while still generalizing to new data.
  • Complex model (low bias, high variance): A high-degree polynomial. It passes through every training point (low bias) but fluctuates wildly when trained on different subsets (high variance).

Sweet Spot: The optimal model complexity sits at the minimum of the total error curve. At this point, neither bias nor variance dominates, and generalization performance is maximized.

Regularization and the Tradeoff

Regularization techniques (L1/Lasso, L2/Ridge) provide a dial to control the tradeoff:

  • Increasing regularization (higher $\alpha$ in Ridge, lower $C$ in SVM) constrains the model, increasing bias but reducing variance.
  • Decreasing regularization allows the model more freedom, reducing bias but increasing variance.

The goal of hyperparameter tuning (e.g., cross-validated grid search) is to find the regularization strength that minimizes total expected error on unseen data.
