Variational Autoencoders: Probabilistic Latents

Lesson Objectives

What you will learn

Understand how VAEs map inputs to probability distributions (Gaussian) rather than deterministic points. Grasp the role of mean $\mu$, log-variance $\log(\sigma^2)$, and why this creates continuous latent spaces.

Prerequisites

Basic probability (Gaussian distributions, mean, variance). Understanding of traditional AE limitations from Lesson 10.4.

Expected Outcome

Describe the VAE probabilistic encoder architecture, explain why sampling from a distribution creates a continuous latent space, and contrast it with deterministic AE encoding.

Lesson 10.4: Introduction to Variational Autoencoders (VAE)

Why VAEs Were Introduced

Traditional autoencoders suffer from a major limitation: their latent space is disorganized and discontinuous. Because there is no constraint on how the bottleneck positions points, the encoder groups points arbitrarily.

If you sample a random point from a gap in the latent space, the decoder will output meaningless noise because it has never seen that region during training. This makes traditional autoencoders useless for generating new data.

The VAE Solution

A Variational Autoencoder (VAE) fixes this. Instead of mapping an input to a single deterministic point in latent space, the encoder maps the input to a probability distribution (a Gaussian prior defined by a mean $\mu$ and a log-variance $\log(\sigma^2)$).

Because the encoder outputs a range of possible values (a distribution) rather than a single coordinate, the latent space becomes continuous. Nearby coordinates in latent space decode to visually similar images, allowing you to generate new samples by interpolating between coordinates.

Probabilistic Encoder Architecture

In a traditional autoencoder, the encoder outputs a single vector $z$ (the latent code). In a VAE, the encoder outputs two vectors describing a Gaussian distribution:

  • $\mu$ (mean vector): The center of the encoded region in latent space.
  • $\log(\sigma^2)$ (log-variance vector): The logarithm of the variance (squared spread) of the distribution. Using log-variance ensures numerical stability and enforces $\sigma^2 > 0$ naturally.

The latent vector $z$ is then sampled from this distribution:

z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2 I)
where $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2 I)$ is a multivariate Gaussian with diagonal covariance.

Why a Gaussian Prior?

The standard normal prior $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$ is chosen for three reasons:

  1. Computational convenience: KL divergence has a closed-form expression with Gaussians.
  2. Centered and scaled: The prior keeps latent coordinates near zero with unit variance, preventing arbitrary drift.
  3. Continuous support: The Gaussian distributes probability mass smoothly over the entire space, eliminating gaps.

Why This Creates Continuous Latent Spaces

Because the encoder outputs a distribution, the loss function forces nearby points in input space to map to overlapping distributions in latent space. Two similar inputs produce Gaussian blobs that overlap. The decoder sees a smooth continuum of latent values during training, teaching it to generate meaningful outputs for any latent coordinate, not just the discrete points seen in traditional autoencoder training.
